<p>If you&#x27;ve worked in sport long enough, you&#x27;ve probably seen a club or a person keeping processes that everyone knows do not make sense.<br><br>Routine meetings that could&#x27;ve been an email. Reports that nobody reads. Tech and data that does not move the needle. Nobody believes these things are making the organisation better. But when it comes to changing or replace them…that feels quite hard.<br><br>Some may say this is due to <em>&quot;fear of the unknown.&quot; </em>I am sure there is some truth in that, but I don&#x27;t think it&#x27;s the main reason.<br><br>When something is familiar, even if it is not good, you’ve already paid the price for it. You know exactly the pain points and you’ve built workarounds. You’ve invested time and energy and now that&#x27;s the way you work.<br><br>And even if it is not great at least you know what you&#x27;re dealing with.<br><br>Switching to something new means starting all over again including the learning curve. And on top of that, there’s no guarantee that the new thing will be better than the old one.<br><br>So people stick with what they know regardless. That&#x27;s sunk-cost thinking in practice. The more time and effort you&#x27;ve invested in a system, the harder it becomes to make changes, even when you are aware about all the issues it has.<br><br>This is common in sports. Poor processes, irrelevant metrics and even staff roles that no longer add any value. You can see they’re costing more than the value they deliver but replacing them means going through a period of short-term chaos.<br><br>From that perspective, showing resistance actually makes sense.</p>

<p><br>I’ve done many presentations about strategy changes in theory and on slides, everyone agrees. The challenge is not convincing others that the new thing is better, most likely they already get that. </p>

<p>The challenge is making the change cost them less.</p>

<p>And there are some things you can do to help with that, I just mention a few of my go-to here:</p>

<p><strong>1. Don’t frame it as scrapping the old stuff.</strong><br>Telling people the current system was wrong is basically telling them their years of hard work were a waste of their time. Nobody likes that. Instead, talk about building on what they already started. <em>“We’re evolving this”</em>, not <em>“We’re getting rid of this”</em>. It helps with people’s pride and makes the transition feel less like a personal loss.</p>

<p><strong>2. Make the first steps reversible.</strong><br>The scariest part isn’t the decision to change something but that awkward in-between phase where you’ve left the old way of doing something but don’t fully trust the new one yet. Start small. For example, introduce the &quot;new thing&quot; with the reserve squad first. Or start the new testing protocol with just a few players instead of the full team. Small, reversible steps make trying something new feel a lot less risky.</p>

<p><strong>3. Don’t ignore the workarounds.</strong><br>People working inside a poor process usually build clever (and often invisible) ways of making it work. <em>The analyst who manually pulls data every Monday because the software doesn’t do it automatically </em>or<em> the physio who keeps a spreadsheet because the team platform can&#x27;t upload key info.</em> If the new process that you want to implement doesn’t account for these invisible patches, people continue to use the old ones. If you replace something make sure to account for the whole ecosystem.</p>

<p><strong>4. Get the timing right.</strong><br>The exact same change can feel ten times harder depending on when you introduce it. Rolling out something new during the chaos of pre-season when everyone’s already pushed beyond their limits is asking for trouble. But implementing the same idea during a quieter block of the season sounds much more manageable. Sometimes the <em>“what”</em> is fine. It’s just the <em>“when”</em> that kills the idea. </p>

<p>Of course, you can also “do nothing”.<br><br>We can choose to do nothing and avoid making changes. But we often underestimate the cost of doing nothing because it is invisible; it is already embedded in the current process. Change, on the other hand, is immediately noticeable and feels disruptive at first, which is why it makes people uncomfortable in the beginning.</p>

<p>Most people in sport already know <em>what</em> needs to change. Many even know <em>why</em>. The problem is not lack of awareness. </p>

<p>It is that replacing the current system is more painful than keeping the old one.</p>

<p>Solve that, and you should see less resistance.</p>
