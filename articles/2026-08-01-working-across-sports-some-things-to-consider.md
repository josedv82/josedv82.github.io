<p>I grew up playing and coaching basketball. Even though I&#x27;ve spent much less of my career working in basketball than in other sports, I&#x27;m still somehow labelled as &quot;<em>the basketball guy</em>&quot; everywhere else I go.</p>

<p>In 2015, I joined the Houston Astros in a Sport Science role. Six years later, I was still referred to as a “<em>non-baseball guy</em>”. Some things never change. And that is exactly one of the main reasons I have been able to work across sports: <em>because some things just do not change</em>.</p>

<p>Most organisations already have plenty of people who know the sport inside out. What they lack is someone who can look at the same problems without the assumptions that come from years inside that sport. You don’t need to be a technical or tactical expert to add value in a performance role. You need to identify what actually drives performance and work with the coaches and athletes who understand the sport better than you do.</p>

<p>Here are some principles that helped me successfully adapt across basketball, baseball and football:</p>

<h2>1. Start with what transfers</h2>

<p>The main concern is thinking that the physical side is going to be different from one sport to another, but this has never been the case in my experience. Let me explain.</p>

<p>Recovery follows the same principles whether you are a pitcher or a basketball player. Force production plays almost always a role regardless of the sport and it tends to be limiting factor in the sport itself or parts of it. Load management and periodisation follow universal principles. </p>

<blockquote><p>The principles transfer. The environment and its constraints determine how you apply them.</p></blockquote>

<p>Understanding these principles and knowing how to apply them in your environment will get you 80% of the way there. The other 20% is the sport-specific part, which takes a little longer and requires observation, experimentation and time.</p>

<h2>2. Understand the problem before solving it</h2>

<p>Understanding the problem comes down to two questions:</p>

<ol><li>What does the sport demand from the player?</li><li>What constraints does the environment impose?</li></ol>

<p>The first question is about understanding what the athlete needs to do to be successful across technical, tactical, physical and mental domains. On the physical side in particular, what efforts are required? At what intensities? How often? What are the positional differences? What does the athlete profile look like? What are the norms? And what does building the physical capacity to meet those demands look like?<br><br>The second refers to the constraints imposed around the sport. How often do we compete? How demanding is the schedule? What factors outside the sport itself influence what we can and cannot do? These constraints are just as important because they provide the context in which practical solutions need to work.</p>

<p>When I first started working in baseball, the research on the demands of the sport was very limited, so we had to use our own environment to objectively profile basic sport demands. Some things we learned through observation and critical thinking. Others required embedding data collection into training sessions and games. Start somewhere and start early but it is important to build an informative environment.</p>

<p>Today there&#x27;s a good chance that when joining an organisation, you&#x27;ll inherit several years of tracking and physical profiling data. We can also measure more aspects of the game than ever before and there is more research available. That helps but nothing truly replicates your environment, your athletes, or your constraints. <a href="https://www.scienceforsport.com/needs-analysis/">Sooner or later, you&#x27;ll need to carry out your own needs analysis.</a></p>

<blockquote><p>The goal is to align first principles with interventions that fit the athletes and environment you are in.</p></blockquote>

<h2>3. Build relationships</h2>

<p>Relationships and human connection determine whether you can have more or less impact. This is true in most fields and performance and sport science is no different.</p>

<p><a href="https://x.com/the_hidden_game/status/2075210981534490883">I&#x27;ve written before about how some principles from Game Theory can help with conversations</a>. Beyond that, here are a few things I&#x27;ve found useful:</p>

<ul><li>Don&#x27;t hide behind a laptop. Nothing is more important than the athlete in the gym or the coach running a training session. If there is a player in the gym or on the pitch, be there. Observe, learn, ask. Everything else, especially admin and computer work, can wait.</li><li>Drop the jargon. Learn the language of the sport quickly. Technical jargon does not help, even if you think it does.</li><li>Suggest. Do not impose. If you still do not get traction, go back and review.</li><li>Don&#x27;t try to fix the sport in one month, not even in one season. <a href="https://x.com/the_hidden_game/status/2074809643096797362?s=20">Focus on early wins</a>. Consistent small improvements.</li></ul>

<p>The ability to build connections is what will open doors to bigger asks in the future</p>

<h2>4. Tech and data processes are universal</h2>

<p>Chances are big part of your role revolves around measuring, using technology and collecting data. And this process is the same regardless of the sport.<br><br>Evaluate the technology, design your data collection strategy. Then decide what, if anything, to do with the information. The skills to do this are universal:</p>

<ul><li>Critical evaluation of validity, reliability, and practical utility.</li><li>Turning data into information that supports decision-making.</li><li>Adding context to numbers and connecting the dots.</li></ul>

<p>Your needs analysis should help determine what is worth measuring or at least what to start with. <a href="https://www.sportsmith.co/listen/pacey-performance-podcast-299-xavi-schelling/">Technology should not create new questions.</a></p>

<h2>5. Think beyond Sport Science</h2>

<p>Sport is much bigger than Sport Science. That does not mean that common scientific principles apply to every part of the performance environment.</p>

<p>Some of the biggest opportunities to create value fall outside the traditional Sport Science job description. Recruitment, skill acquisition, and practice design have plenty of low-hanging fruit because performance principles have not been applied as systematically. I&#x27;d even say these are the areas with the highest return on investment, both financially and competitively.<br><br>One small advice I&#x27;d give to a sport scientist is to detach yourself from routine tasks that can be automated or delegated. Data uploads, report updates and many other administrative tasks consume a lot of time and create zero value. The idea should be to design those processes so they demand as less of your time and attention as possible, freeing yourself to focus more on areas where you can have more impact.<br><br>Of course, there is a time and place for those tasks, particularly early in your career or in smaller organisations. I am not saying to avoid them entirely, but to build them in a way that helps progressively free up your time so you can spend more of it supporting other areas which will also enable you to expand on your role. </p>

<h2>Bottom line</h2>

<p>Sport-specific knowledge is valuable, but most organisations already have a lot of it. When they hire someone from outside the sport, they do so because they believe that this person can help improve the organisation.</p>

<p>A performance role has less to do with the sport itself and more with helping build processes that move the organisation forward and hopefully, make it smarter. That requires a solid understanding of human physiology and training principles, a structured approach to understanding the sport and the environment, the ability to build strong relationships, effective use of technology and data to support decision-making and showing the curiosity to contribute wherever you can create the greatest value.</p>

<p>Develop those, stay humble and keep the coaches and athletes at the centre of everything you do, and you&#x27;ll be able to add meaningful value in almost any sport. Even more effectively than someone who is very experienced in one sport but lighter on the scientific and interpersonal side.</p>
