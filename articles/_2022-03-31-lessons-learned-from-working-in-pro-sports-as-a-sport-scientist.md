# Lessons learned from working in pro sports as a sport scientist

A few years ago I shared ten things I had learned working in pro sport as a sport scientist. It was a visual thread, with a short thought and an image for each one.

Some were about training and recovery. Others were about data, hiring or work-life balance. Quite different topics, but many of the problems behind them are connected.

I'll start with something that sounds obvious: you won't have to sell your work so much if you can show how it connects to performance.

As practitioners, we spend a lot of time learning the technical side of things. That's necessary. But when you are trying to get a coach or a player to buy into something, explaining how good the method is only gets you so far. They need to understand what it helps them do.

If you are asking a player to do extra work, how does that help him on the pitch? If you want the coach to change part of the training week, what problem does it solve? These are reasonable questions. And sometimes we spend more time defending our methods than answering them.

This also applies to recovery.

I wrote that recovery is more about training than about resting. Of course, rest matters. But before looking for another recovery intervention, we should probably look at what we are asking the athlete to recover from.

How are we organising the work? Are we constantly trying to compensate for training decisions that could have been better in the first place?

It is easy to keep adding things after training. Changing the training itself can be a much more difficult conversation.

And health is a requirement. A player needs to be available to train and compete. But availability alone doesn't tell us whether we are preparing him well for what he needs to do in the game. We still need to make that connection.

There is a similar problem with data.

Collect data for the things you can't see. That was another point in the thread. There are good reasons to measure things that are difficult to observe, or changes that happen slowly enough that you might miss them. But we also collect plenty of information that adds very little to what the people working with the athlete already know.

Even when a number is easy to collect, someone has to organise it, interpret it and put it in a report. Then someone else has to read that report.

Before adding another measure, ask yourself what you would lose if you didn't have it. Sometimes the answer is quite important. In other cases, you may struggle to come up with one.

There is no shortage of questions in sport. Learn the skills to answer them. That could mean getting better at analysing data, understanding the sport or simply asking the coach a better question.

Problem solve first. Innovation will come naturally.

The environment is already complex enough. Different departments, different priorities, competition schedules and very little time. Whatever solution you introduce has to work inside all of that.

The more complex the environment, the easier the solution should be to use. There can be a lot of work behind a simple recommendation. That is fine. But if the person using it needs to understand everything you did before they can make a decision, there is probably more work to do on your side.

---

Another lesson was about self-protection.

Hiring someone who is better than you at part of your job can feel uncomfortable. You may have to give up some responsibilities, change your role or accept that something could have been done better.

But avoiding that person doesn't make the gap go away. It just means the department keeps it.

Hire the best people you can and make adjustments if you have to.

The same applies to processes you have built. Advantages are temporary. Something that gave you an advantage a few years ago may now be common practice, or may no longer be useful in your current environment.

That doesn't mean the work was wrong. But you need to go back and review it. Otherwise, the process stays because everyone is used to it and changing it would be inconvenient.

We can become quite attached to things we have spent time building. So it is worth checking whether they still help, even when nobody is asking us to change them.

---

And then there is work-life balance.

This is probably the part some people will disagree with most. Working in professional sport is demanding, and those demands affect the people around you too. The schedule doesn't care very much about what else you had planned that week.

In the thread I put it quite bluntly: get in, sprint for as long as you want, then get out. Ensure those around you are onboard.

You can decide that a period of your career is worth that commitment. But be honest about what it involves. How long do you want to do it? What are the people around you being asked to accept? And have you actually talked about it with them?

There isn't one answer that works for everyone. But these are conversations worth having before you realise the job has been making those decisions for you.

I'm sure some of these points are still controversial. I'd be interested to hear which ones people with experience in the field would change, or what they would add.
