<p>Back in 2013, the first time I met <a href="https://x.com/@spikesonly">@spikesonly</a> in person at a bar in Boston, he dropped a quote I had heard many times before and in many different ways, but somehow that one stayed with me: </p>

<blockquote><p><em>&quot;You are only as good as what you can get your athletes to do.&quot;</em> </p></blockquote>

<p>As a sport scientist or coach, you spend years learning the technical side of things. We learn biomechanics, programming, nutrition, rehabilitation, physiology, training theory, GPS, force plates... degrees, masters, courses, certifications, all because we believe that the better the technical knowledge, the better we are as practitioners.</p>

<p>And that is true. Technical knowledge is the entry level. You need to develop it throughout your career. And if you are not good at it then this article is irrelevant. You need to start there. </p>

<p>That said, influence determines how much of that knowledge you actually get to apply.</p>

<p>I&#x27;ve worked in both environments, academies and pro teams. Academies are relatively easier in the sense that you can be more authoritarian. You decide what training you do, what tests you run, when and how often, and players do it without much hesitation. It is not that education does not matter at this level, but the risk of resistance is much lower. When you make the switch to a pro-environment or a first team, the first thing you notice is that players have a lot more agency to decide whether or not they want to do what you suggest. And it is not just agency. In many leagues they are protected and regulated by law, and you have to abide by that.</p>

<p>So your technical knowledge becomes worthless half the time because you cannot even start to implement what you want.</p>

<p>I&#x27;ve been there, and there is not a season where I did not come across a player (often more than one), who does not believe in lifting heavy in season. Or not even in season. Some just do not want to lift anywhere near what they should to drive adaptations. I&#x27;ve had players refuse to run linearly through a speed gate out of fear of pulling a hamstring. I&#x27;ve had players who do not want to do a Nordic because they do not like the feeling. That three second single leg iso squat? Forget it, too heavy on their back. They come in all flavours, and I am sure you&#x27;ve tasted them all.</p>

<p>The mistake I made and still make sometime, is trying to convince through science and knowledge. </p>

<p>It just does not work, and it never will. Persuasion is a skill. You need to learn how to negotiate, convince and how to move others toward what you are trying to achieve. And at least in my case, I never received any formal training on how to do this.</p>

<p>Some coaches I&#x27;ve worked with are naturally good at this. Others, like me, are just terrible. But there are things I picked up along the way and I will try to explain them here through practical examples.</p>

<h2>First, connect it to performance</h2>

<p>This one is the obvious one. <br><br>In most team sports, the performance department is a support function, not the main event. Before you even think about convincing players and coaches to buy into your training, you need to connect what you&#x27;re asking them to do with what matters most:<em> improving performance in the sport itself.</em></p>

<p>If you&#x27;re asking your centre-back to squat at 90% of their 1RM on MD-4, you should be able to explain exactly why. Don&#x27;t just tell them it&#x27;s &quot;<em>good for strength.</em>&quot; </p>

<p>You need to be able to show how becoming stronger relative to their body weight can help them accelerate faster over the first 5–30 metres, which then will allow them to play a higher defensive line becomes because they have a better chance of recovering if the ball is played in behind.</p>

<p>When players understand that this is about the sport, not about the gym, chances are they will be more interested. If you do not have any data to back it up, then you better start there because you&#x27;ll need it at some point.</p>

<h2>Good...but that&#x27;s not enough</h2>

<p>No, it&#x27;s not. Connecting what you do to performance is essential, but unfortunately it isn&#x27;t always enough.</p>

<p>If you&#x27;re convinced that the work you want a player to do will benefit them, but they don&#x27;t want to do it or would rather do something else, then you&#x27;re no longer dealing with a training problem. You and the player are now playing a game. You each have your own objectives and every decision changes what happens next. Once you see it this way, a lot of difficult conversations suddenly become much easier to think about.</p>

<p>Now you can start identifying your best options and anticipating the player&#x27;s responses and thinking through the likely outcome of each decision. </p>

<p>Nothing I&#x27;ll discuss in the following sections is novel. These are well-established ideas from game theory and behavioural psychology that have helped me not only have better conversations with the athletes and coaches I&#x27;ve worked with but also become more effective at influencing the behaviours that ultimately, I wanted to implement.</p>

<p>Let&#x27;s go one by one.</p>

<h2>The repeated game</h2>

<p>Imagine that you are working with a player just for one week. You are not going to see him again after. If he asks you to skip the gym today even if you think he should do it, you are more likely to agree because there are no big consequences after this week.</p>

<p>Now back to our reality. In team sports like football, you probably have somewhere between 250-300 training days in a season, many of those will have gym sessions scheduled.</p>

<p>So if the player asks to skip the gym today, the decision is not only about today. If you concede easily players know they can negotiate. If you are strict on holding your line, they&#x27;ll think you never care about their opinions. So you need to find the balance between both.</p>

<p>In game theory this is called <em>&quot;repeated game&quot;</em>. In reality, this is what good coaches do instinctively without calling it anything. They will give a player a small win on purpose, like letting him skip the gym today if we can afford it, so that the day where the gym is more important, the player remembers you listened.</p>

<p>A player who feels heard will likely trust and follow you more. And in the long term that is more important. Trust is built over time through multiple interactions, each influencing the next one.</p>

<h2>Cheap Talk</h2>

<p>There is one thing I used to say every time I ran a Yo-Yo test before training: &quot;<em>trust me, you&#x27;ll be tired for a minute when you finish, but it won&#x27;t affect the rest of the session.</em>&quot;... I had many variations of it.</p>

<p>Looking back, that is probably one of the least convincing things I could have said. From the player&#x27;s side, I am the one who decided to include the test, and the one who scheduled it right before training. Of course I am going to say it won&#x27;t affect them. My words there cost me nothing. I would say the exact same thing whether it was true or not, so the player has no reason to weigh it.</p>

<p>That is what game theory calls <em>cheap talk</em>. Talk that costs the speaker nothing carries almost nothing to someone whose interests do not line up with his. You can say it louder or with more conviction, but your words are still free to you.</p>

<p><strong>Skin in the game</strong></p>

<p>So instead of asking him to trust me, I put something on the line. Something like: &quot;<em>if you still feel tired in the first part of training, I&#x27;ll go to the coach and get your volume cut in the last drill.</em>&quot;</p>

<p>Now the words cost me. If I am wrong, I am the one who has to walk over to the coach and change the plan in front of everyone. That is the difference. Now I have something to pay for if I am wrong.</p>

<p>The more your words cost you if you turn out to be wrong, the more the player will consider them.</p>

<h2>Screening</h2>

<p>Before you try to convince a player of anything, you need to know what the root of the problem is, and that starts with knowing your players.</p>

<p>When you get an <em>&quot;I don&#x27;t want to do it&quot;</em>, you may be dealing with two different situations. One is genuine, the player really does not want to do something, maybe because it is painful or he knows he will be sore the next day. The other is the excuse, <em>&quot;I just don&#x27;t fancy it today&quot;.</em> If you know your players you can sometimes guess which is which, but game theory also has something called &quot;<em>screening&quot;</em> that is useful here.</p>

<p>As a coach, you should try to understand whether there are real concerns you should know about. It&#x27;s not just about convincing a player into something. You should be genuinely interested in giving them the training you believe is best for them.</p>

<p>So, to help with this you set a screening question. &quot;<em>What does it do to you, exactly?&quot;</em></p>

<p>Take the player who refuses to run linearly through the speed gates because he is scared of his hamstring. Ask him and see the depth of his answer. If he tells you the hamstring goes tight at top speed, that he has felt it before, that it happened in a specific game then there is some depth there you can start working with. The player who just does not fancy it today will give you a much thinner answer because he is just trying to get out of it.</p>

<p>Screening helps you discover which problem is in front of you. In general, you do not want to concede to the player giving you an excuse and push the one with an honest concern.</p>

<h2>Private Information</h2>

<p>Now the screening section reads a bit as if you are just trying to catch the player making an excuse. But it is not about that.</p>

<p>Go back to the speed gates example. The player told you his hammy gets tight at top speed, that he has felt it before during a game. On the other hand, you have data (his GPS, his Nordic, etc). But what you do not have is the thing he actually feels when he gets to top speed. He knows something about the inside of his own body that does not show up in your data.</p>

<p>Game theory calls this <em>private information</em>. The player knows something the coach does not. I made the mistake before to assume that I always had better information because I had the numbers. I was wrong. His numbers might look perfectly clean, yet he may feel something is not right with his hamstring.</p>

<p>So the screening question can help you stay grounded and honest. Sometimes the answer you get is a warning you need to pay attention to. The player who tells you his hamstring is not right might be saving you from the injury that is three sprints away.</p>

<p>Everything in this article is about moving a player toward what you believe is best for him. <em>Private information</em> is the reminder that sometimes he is the one who is right and good coaches know when to stop persuading him and change the plan.</p>

<h2>Give the choice back</h2>

<p>Let&#x27;s say that you&#x27;ve gone through all the above steps. We connected the work to performance, we screened the concern and listened to the player, and after all that you still believe the player should do the thing you planned but he does not want to. Now what?</p>

<p>One option is to push harder. Pull authority and force the player. But this is likely the thing that will hurt you the most.</p>

<p>You take away a choice from someone and all of a sudden they feel the need to stand their ground even more. Now you are telling them what to do, probably the thing they hate the most.</p>

<p>You have created a second reason to say no, and this one has nothing to do with training. So even if you win, you have compromised every future interaction with this player.</p>

<p>Your other option (and probably your best one) is to give the decision back to the player, making sure he knows everything he needs to know. <em>&quot;Here is what this is for. Here is what we are going to watch. If it does not feel right, we stop. Your call.&quot;</em></p>

<p>You are not giving up the plan and you are not pretending you do not have an opinion. You are explicitly telling him where you stand while leaving the decision with him. And for me this is the smart move, because you are removing the fight over being forced to do it and that fight can be very damaging.</p>

<p>Up until now I discussed principles pulled from <em>game theory</em>. This one actually comes from psychology, from what is called &quot;<em>reactance&quot;</em>. People are more likely to push back the moment a choice is taken away from them. I thought it was worth mentioning it here because I tend to resource to it quite a bit.</p>

<p>When you leave the choice in the player&#x27;s hand it can feel like you are losing control, but it is the opposite. </p>

<p>A player who is forced into something will do it at 50%. The player who chooses to do it is more likely to do it properly, because he chose to. Either way you want the second player, because if he is only going to do it at 50%, may as well not do it at all.</p>

<h2>In the end</h2>

<p>I want to finish with a couple of thoughts.</p>

<p>First, to reiterate that nothing I mentioned here is new. Coaches have been using many of these principles for a long time. The idea with this article is to bring awareness and put a name to them, so that if anybody wants to learn more, this is a good start.</p>

<p>Second, I spent years thinking I needed to be right, have all the answers, collect all the data, find the study that proved my point. And that is just half of what I needed to become a good coach. We&#x27;ve all heard that coaching is <em>a mix of art and science</em>. For me the <em>art</em> part has always been the hard part.</p>

<p>Hopefully this article helps anybody who is in the same position I&#x27;ve been in and still am sometimes.</p>
