<p>Pick one player...let’s call him <em>Player X</em>. </p>

<p>Go around and separately ask five different people what he needs to improve most right now: the strength coach, the technical coach, the scout, the academy coordinator and the head of performance. </p>

<p>If you get five different answers you’ve just found both, your biggest development opportunity <em>and </em>your biggest organizational problem at the same time. </p>

<p>Players don’t develop in departmental boxes. They don’t experience growth as “physical,” “technical” or “mental” in isolation. They experience it on the pitch during training and games, in specific actions.</p>

<p>That’s why a player will rarely buy into the extra weight on the barbell unless he clearly understands how it is going to improve his game.</p>

<p>Departments tend to optimise around their own activities. The gym has its targets, the technical staff has theirs, the psychologist has his. And at the end, the player ends up with a collection of disconnected pieces rather than a coherent plan. </p>

<p>The goal is not to lift more weight...that&#x27;s the tool. The real goal is a specific performance outcome on the pitch. Once that outcome is crystal clear, you can reverse-engineer everything else. Each department knows exactly what it needs to do to contribute and how it fits into the bigger picture around that player. </p>

<p>Without a shared development goal, you get competing priorities, mixed messages and a player caught in the middle trying to piece it all together.  </p>

<p>A clear, common development goal (or goals) changes that. It gives everyone (regardless of their specialty) something to align around and it turns a group of coaches into an actual player development <em>system</em>.  </p>

<p>Development goals don’t belong to one discipline or department. They belong to the actions the player has to perform while playing the sport.</p>
