<p>Forget football for a second.</p>

<p>Imagine you’re running a venture capital fund. You have hundreds of opportunities to invest and each of those investments is fighting for the same limited capital and resources. You have to place your bet five or ten years before you know whether any of your calls will be right.</p>

<p>That&#x27;s pretty similar to how academy recruitment works in football.</p>

<p>That may sound like a cliche analogy, but a football academy is like a fund, the players are the portfolio, and the squad place is the capital you can only spend once. As soon as you start to understand this, the questions start changing.</p>

<p>A fund manager doesn’t only ask <em>“is this a good company?”</em> He asks, <em>“is the market pricing this company correctly?”</em> </p>

<p>Clubs invest lots of effort trying to identify better players but much less trying to understand the market those players come from. Every academy is searching for better players. But how many are asking whether the market is valuing those players correctly? That&#x27;s where competitive advantages are.</p>

<p>So, while you read this article make sure you wear your economist hat and we will use common financial terms to describe talent development dynamics.</p>

<h2>Information asymmetry</h2>

<p>An economist would start with <em>information asymmetry</em>. Markets break down when the buyer can’t see what an asset is really worth.</p>

<p>If you ever tried to buy a second-hand car you have probably noticed that the seller has an advantage over you. He knows the car much better than you do. Its flaws, whether the engine has any faults or not. This is the exact problem Akerlof used to show how a market can rot from the inside (<a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1879431">Akerlof, 1970</a>).</p>

<p>That gap between what the seller knows and what the buyer knows is the source of many bad deals (for the buyer).</p>

<p>Football academies live inside that gap permanently. Nobody can see the future quality of a 15-year-old. All you see is today’s performance, and today’s performance is never pure talent. It’s talent mixed with growth, strength, power, speed, confidence, games played and so on.</p>

<p>The thing you’re trying to value is half hidden.</p>

<p>And when a market can’t see value it starts trading on <em>&quot;signals&quot;</em>.</p>

<h2>Signalling</h2>

<p>So what counts as a <em>signal</em>?</p>

<p>When it comes to young football players there are obvious signals...the kid who dominates the age group, the one who scores frequently, the one who dribbles past defenders easier than others, the one who wins duels... you name it.</p>

<p>Of course, all that is football, but guess what maturation does to that list? It inflates almost every item on it. Bigger, faster, stronger kids generate louder signals regardless of underlying talent, so the market quietly starts mistaking visibility for value. When we have limited information markets tend to move towards the strongest available signals.</p>

<p>Signalling theory is the idea that buyers lean on observable proxies when they can’t see the thing itself (<a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1882010">Spence, 1973</a>). Football just happens to be really good at this.</p>

<h2>Information cascades</h2>

<p>The same kid that we described before now gets the call up for the regional team or even the youth national team.</p>

<p>Now all of a sudden, three good academies start tracking the same physically dominant kid. And a fourth academy starts asking about the kid as well and sending a scout to watch him live.</p>

<p>Nothing changed. The only new information was that other clubs had shown interest.</p>

<p>That’s an <em>information cascade</em>, where people rationally copy the choices in front of them and ignore their own read (<a href="https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261849">Bikhchandani et al., 1992</a>).</p>

<p>Many years before, J.M. Keynes illustrated this with a newspaper competition where readers had to pick the prettiest face from a set of photographs. The winner wasn’t the person who chose the face they personally found most attractive. It was the person who best predicted which face everyone else would choose. Success came from correctly predicting what everyone else would do. (<a href="https://archive.org/details/generaltheoryofe00keyn/page/n5/mode/2up">Keynes, 1936</a>).</p>

<p>Youth recruitment works just like that. The question stops being <em>“will this player become elite?”</em> and becomes <em>“will everyone else believe he will?”</em></p>

<h2>Adverse selection</h2>

<p>Once enough clubs agree a player is special, demand climbs, competition climbs, expectations climb, and his price climbs with them.</p>

<p>Meanwhile a kid who might be technically better or have a better game IQ but is two years behind physically gets moved down to the bottom of the depth chart. All while his actual potential hasn’t changed at all.</p>

<p>That’s what’s called <em>adverse selection</em>: the strongest signals get bid up, the quiet value gets driven out (<a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1879431">Akerlof, 1970</a>).</p>

<p>In one study, researchers tracked the biological maturity of players across an English academy. The bias towards physically advanced players increased year after year until, by the under-15 and under-16 squads, there wasn’t a single late maturer left (<a href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2019.1649524">Hill et al., 2020</a>). Late developers get filtered out as the market continued rewarding the strongest signals.</p>

<p>On the other hand, if you look at the top level the bias in youth teams starts to reverse. A study of English rugby union pathways (a sport where “<em>he’s just bigger</em>” is an even more seductive signal than in football), found that disproportionately more early-born players drop out before the top, leaving the later-born survivors overrepresented among those who actually make it (<a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.640607/full">Kelly et al.</a>). The market overpays for the early signal, then watches the cohort it underpriced outperform. In finance, that’s called a <em>pricing error, i</em>n football it&#x27;s just <em>talent identification.</em></p>

<h2>Present bias and market failure</h2>

<p>At this point you’d probably be asking yourself: <em>“ok, so if we know maturation bias exists, why doesn’t the market correct itself?”</em></p>

<p>Simple answer: <em>Incentives</em>.</p>

<p>Academy directors get judged now. When the youth national team calls two players from your academy, that’s a sign your system is working.</p>

<p>Coaches often get judged in even shorter timelines. The league title, the youth cup, and so on.</p>

<p>Parents are constantly comparing environments to find the one that increases the chances for their kids becoming professional.</p>

<p>Everyone talks about the long term but is measured in the short term.</p>

<p><em>Present bias</em> is the habit of overweighting the payoff you can have today against a larger one you’d have to wait for (<a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/116981">O’Donoghue &amp; Rabin, 1999</a>). It means a near-certain return today beats an uncertain one in five years nearly every time.</p>

<p>So if you are an academy, backing the physically dominant player is the smart (or safe) move. Which brings us to the conclusion that when every academy behaves rationally, the market as a whole behaves irrationally.</p>

<p>This is called <em>market failure</em> (<a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/1882231">Bator, 1958</a>).</p>

<h2>Diversification</h2>

<p>Finance has one more lesson football rarely borrows: <em>diversification</em>.</p>

<p>Good investors don’t fill their portfolios with the assets everyone already agrees are valuable. They spread their investments across different profiles to deal with uncertainty (<a href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x?msockid=2f6b6f0b979861c223107d15964a6033">Markowitz, 1952</a>). Some investments generate quick returns; others take years to realise their value. Some are reliable while others carry greater upside. In any case, success is measured by the performance of the portfolio as a whole not just one stock.</p>

<p>Now look at a typical academy intake: Physically advanced. Early developers. Immediate performers.  If a fund manager told you ninety percent of his portfolio was concentrated in the same type of company, you’d question his strategy. Yet that’s often how academy squads are built.</p>

<h2>Real options</h2>

<p>Another concept borrowed from finance: <em>real options</em>.</p>

<p>When investors pay a small amount today just to preserve the possibility of a much larger future payoff. In other words, reserving the right to benefit if the opportunity eventually proves valuable (<a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0304405X77900150">Myers, 1977</a>).</p>

<p>Keeping a late maturer is exactly that kind of decision. Sean Cumming, (<a href="https://x.com/@phd_sean">@phd_sean</a>), a world-leading expert in the science of growth and maturation, describes Gareth Bale as a classic example (<a href="https://podiumanalytics.org/news/the-science-that-saved-harry-kanes-career/">Cumming, n.d.</a>). During a rapid adolescent growth spurt, Bale temporarily lost coordination, his performances declined and Southampton questioned whether to offer him a scholarship. Instead of assuming this was lack of talent, the academy staff recognised it as a temporary consequence of maturation, adapted his training and gave him time to adjust. Within months, he had returned to his previous level and eventually went on to be one of the world’s best players.</p>

<p>Economically, the cost of keeping him for another year was the premium paid to preserve the opportunity. Releasing him at 15 would have been the only irreversible decision in the entire process. Once the option disappears you can’t buy it back. <br><br>Gareth Bale isn&#x27;t the only example. He&#x27;s just one of the few we know about. The players who never made it because they were released too early don&#x27;t get documentaries, interviews or transfer records. Their stories disappear the moment they are released.</p>

<h2>Arbitrage</h2>

<p><em>Arbitrage</em> is when someone recognises that the market has priced an asset incorrectly and positioning yourself before everyone else catches up.</p>

<p>Parts of football have already started responding. Bio-banding (grouping players by biological maturity rather than chronological age) exists because some academies recognised that age-group competition was distorting how talent was being evaluated.</p>

<p>Southampton were early adopters; the Premier League has trialled bio-banded competitions and clubs such as Brentford have incorporated maturation into their development pathways (<a href="https://www.sportsmith.co/articles/bio-banding-brentford-fc-academy-players-age-is-just-another-number/">Sportsmith</a>). And more initiatives are being implemented internationally as we speak.</p>

<p>This is an attempt to correct a market distortion.</p>

<p>Concepts like bio-banding or implementing better player development pathways with calibrated KPIs and evaluation criteria at the academy level is a step in the right direction.</p>

<p>But the lesson here is that markets can be wrong. And wrong markets create opportunities.</p>

<p>That doesn’t mean filling your academy with late maturers. That would just mean replacing one bias with another. </p>

<p>Most academies are trying to identify better players. The biggest competitive advantage may come from identifying where the market is consistently wrong.<br><br></p>
